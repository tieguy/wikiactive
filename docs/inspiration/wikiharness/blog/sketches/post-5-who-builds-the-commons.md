# Post 5 Sketch: Who Might Be Interested in Building the Commons?

*Draft after posts 1–4 land; learnings from those will reshape this. Destination: blog/ (alongside the post 4 sketch).*

## Opening move — disambiguate the question

The commons is for everyone. That's the prime objective and it doesn't move. What's open is a different question: *who might be interested in building it*, and what do they need from a harness in order to do that work well?

This is where post 4's traffic-collapse argument gets sharpened. The reader question and the contributor question often get conflated, but they're different. AI disintermediation eats certain kinds of *reading* (fact-lookup, overview — the Singer et al. taxonomy is useful scaffolding here, even though it's about consumption). The contributor question is its own thing: who shows up to build, and what would let them build at meaningfully higher quality and scale than they can today?

A harness is a tool for builders. The post is about who those builders might be.

## Stinson as central precedent

Alex Stinson, "An Organizer's Perspective Part I: 'Anyone can edit' is not a strategy for growing the Wikimedia movement" (Diff, April 2022). Walk through the six New Editor Experiences personas (Wikimedia Foundation + Reboot, 2017–18 research):

- Reactive Corrector
- Knowledge Sharer
- Audience Builder
- Box Checker
- Social Changer
- Joiner Inner

Stinson's claim: tooling improvements have served Reactive Correctors and Knowledge Sharers (the personas with intrinsic compulsion to edit). The other four need *something beyond tools*: financial/promotional goal, outside requirement, vision for change, community to join.

Rhetorical posture: someone with movement standing already made the case that "anyone can edit" is not a strategy. We're extending the same critique into a new era, not picking a fight.

## Personas → harness implications

For each persona, what kind of harness does it imply?

- **Reactive Corrector** — quick low-friction fixes; safety and traceability for small corrections. Closest to the boring MVP. Arguably already well-served.
- **Knowledge Sharer** — externalize held expertise with proper sourcing. Harness as expertise-articulation aid.
- **Audience Builder** — wants visibility. Awkward fit — harness as self-promotion vector? Worth naming and setting aside.
- **Box Checker** — external requirement (assignment, grant, professional duty). Harness should integrate with that context (gradebooks, deliverables, institutional reporting). *This is the WikiEd-shaped opening.*
- **Social Changer** — wants knowledge to change the world. Harness should surface gaps that matter, lower the wikitext barrier. *This is the journalism / civic-research-shaped opening.*
- **Joiner Inner** — wants community. Harness should connect contributors with shared goals and visible collective progress. *This is the WikiProject-shaped opening, but harnessed.*

## 2025 Successful Newcomers Survey as marker

WMF in 2025 is still gathering baseline data on who actually persists past 25 edits in their first 6 months. The survey is described as "starting somewhere" — no broader newcomer survey has been done before. The contributor question is open, the Foundation knows it, the harness era is unfolding while answers are still being gathered.

Don't claim to have the answer. Position the post as joining a live inquiry with a sharper version of the question.

(Also worth noting: WMF Research is concurrently revising the Singer reader taxonomy in 2025 — both halves of the audience picture are being redone right now because of AI.)

## The audience pivot, recast

The earlier "scholars vs. students vs. journalists" framing was directionally right but under-justified — and the framing itself was slightly off. The question isn't *which audience the commons serves* (the commons serves everyone). It's *which kinds of contributors a harness is best suited to invite and amplify*. With the personas in hand it becomes clearer:

- The "learned-contributor" pivot (scholars + students) maps to **Box Checkers** with high-quality external requirements + **Knowledge Sharers** who happen to be experts.
- The journalism / civic-research pivot maps to **Social Changers** with professional accountability.
- WikiEd is the existing proof that designing for Box Checkers with strong institutional support produces durable contribution.
- Agents and downstream systems are *not* in the persona framework. That's a real gap, but probably not "contributors" in the sense Stinson's framework meant — they're closer to consumers-with-feedback-loops. Worth flagging as something the persona framework doesn't capture and a harness era needs to think about separately.

## Closing reframe

The harness shouldn't try to serve all six personas equally. Choosing which personas to design for is itself the strategic move.

The punch: the boring MVP from post 4 implicitly designs for Reactive Correctors and Knowledge Sharers — the personas already best served by current tooling. That's part of why it feels boring.

A bolder MVP commits to other personas. Post 6 then becomes: which candidate hubs best support which personas, and what experiments would tell us whether that commitment is justified.

## Framing note: the governed factory (vs. Grokipedia)

The "factory model" from agentic-engineering writing (the contributor's output becomes the *system that produces the output*, not the output itself) is tempting to reject as anti-commons — it sounds like knowledge production at scale with minimal human touch, i.e. Grokipedia. That rejection is too fast.

The real axis isn't automation vs. handcraft; it's *governed-and-contestable* vs. *captured*. Grokipedia's problem isn't that a factory produces it — it's that one company decides what the factory makes, with no community, no contestation, no transparency. A factory built, tested, and argued over by a community with integrity can carry that integrity into the infrastructure itself.

The commons already proves this. ClueBot NG, ORES, the edit filter / AbuseFilter system are community-built, community-governed automation that encodes community values about what good contribution looks like. The community already runs factories and accepts them — *when they're built and governed in the open*. The question this project raises is whether that governed-automation surface can expand from "revert vandalism" toward "help build and verify claims" without losing the governance that makes it legitimate.

This connects to criterion 3 (governable at the edit level) and to the deferred governance theme. It may deserve fuller treatment — possibly its own post, possibly a thread inside this one.
